Morning — Why Decision Models Exist
🎯
Core Thesis
Prediction Is Not a Decision
▾

The single most important idea of the course: a forecast is not an action. No prediction is 100% accurate — non-zero error is universal — so you need a second, prescriptive model to convert the forecast into the optimal decision.

The wrong (common) practice

Take the ML forecast, add a 10% buffer, call that the order quantity. This ignores the cost asymmetry between over- and under-ordering and the full shape of the error distribution.

The two-step process used in real projects

  1. Predictive model → generates the demand forecast (regression, random forest, LLMs)
  2. Prescriptive/optimization model → translates forecast + error distribution into the optimal decision

Real case: flash-sale online retailer

Client's team predicted demand for new items with ML on 10–12 features (brand, price, CPU, memory, battery…) and used the point prediction (e.g., 200 units) directly as the order quantity. The professor's team kept the same prediction quality but added an optimization layer — decision quality beat the client's by a large margin. Same forecast, better decision.

Alibaba now uses LLMs on proprietary data for demand prediction — but the prescriptive layer is still what converts it to money.

Real case: Deutsche Bank wealth management

  • Hundreds of millions in daily trading volume; regulation requires covering 95% of daily cash redemptions
  • Too much cash held → opportunity cost; too little → expensive market borrowing
  • Predictive insight: redemptions spike at month start/end and around holidays — but even sophisticated ML gave poor point predictions due to volatility
  • Fix: predictive model for demand + inventory-style optimization for the cash level → saved hundreds of millions annually
Takeaway

Data and prediction are necessary but not sufficient. The prescriptive model is what drives the value.

Method
The Seven-Step Decision Modeling Process
▾
  1. Define the problem — the most important step
  2. Collect data needed to solve that problem
  3. Build the formal decision model
  4. Solve the model
  5. Validate — does the solution make sense? does it beat current practice?
  6. Sensitivity analysis — how does the solution change as inputs vary?
  7. Implement and deploy

Problem definition = 3 components

  • Decisions — what is genuinely under your control
  • Performance metric — how success is defined and measured
  • Constraints — what restricts feasible choices

Iteration is expected: stakeholders reveal hidden constraints only after seeing a draft solution ("we can't do that"). Faculty-scheduling preferences surface only after the first draft schedule circulates.

Amazon lesson — the project that died at step 1

A mathematically optimal flexible warehouse-routing model was rejected by the head of logistics: existing contracts locked regions to specific warehouses. "Decisions to be made" were defined too broadly and the whole project went unused. Always confirm what's actually under control before modeling.

Steps 3–4 are increasingly automatable by AI. Steps 1 and 5 — definition and validation — are where human judgment adds the most value.

Linear Programming
📐
Foundations
Linear Programming — The Workhorse
▾

Every LP has three parts: decision variables (what you control), a linear objective (max/min a SUMPRODUCT), and linear constraints. "Linear" = no variables multiplied together, no squares, no IF statements.

Key vocabulary (exam-grade definitions)

TermMeaning
Feasible solutionSatisfies all constraints — but may not be optimal
Optimal solutionFeasible and best objective value
Multiple optimaDifferent decision values, same objective value
Binding constraintUsed to its limit; non-zero shadow price
Shadow priceMarginal value of relaxing a constraint by one unit
Scale of practice

95% of Fortune 500 companies solve LPs every day; commercial solvers handle tens to hundreds of millions of variables.

💼
Worked Case · GreenField
Bond Portfolio LP — $150M Allocation
▾

Investment committee allocates $150M across corporate bonds. Yields range 3.4%–7.5% (higher yield ⇒ higher default risk, 0.5%–30% PD; recovery rates vary).

Model

  • Decisions: portfolio weight per bond (continuous, ≥0, sum to 100%)
  • Objective: maximize weighted average yield = SUMPRODUCT(yields, weights)
  • Constraints: below-investment-grade (B/B+/BB) ≤ 25% · long-maturity (≥10y) ≥ 30% · Σw = 1
  • Flag columns: =IF(OR(D2="B",D2="BB",D2="B+"),1,0) and =IF(H2>=10,1,0), then SUMPRODUCT with weights

What the solution taught

  • Without a per-name cap the LP concentrated in just 3 bonds — feasible, optimal, terrible practice
  • Adding a 20% per-company cap cost only 0.05% of yield (5.445% → 5.395%) for real diversification
  • Sector caps (35–40%) are the next layer

Sensitivity (what to tell the committee)

  • Risky cap 25% → 26% buys ≈ +0.025% yield
  • Long-maturity floor 30% → 25% buys ≈ +0.5% yield — the expensive constraint
Solved in class — optimal 5.395%

Software .20 · Telecom .20 · Auto Mfr .20 · Green Energy .10 · Construction .15 · Luxury .15 — all three policy constraints binding. Green Energy pulls double duty (risky and long-maturity). Tomorrow: Monte Carlo on this portfolio to expose the default-risk tail the LP ignores.

Revenue Management
✈️
Worked Case · VacationAir
Revenue Management — Airline Seat Allocation
▾

The history (great exam color)

  • 1978 Airline Deregulation Act: before, government set fares (NYC–LA round trip ≈ $1,400 inflation-adjusted); after, 200+ airlines entered, fares fell 40%+
  • People Express (1981) → 5th-largest US carrier in 4 years → collapsed 1986 after American's 1985 "Ultimate Super Saver" fares
  • Discount restrictions (30-day advance, non-refundable, Saturday-night stay) were engineered to exclude business travelers — price discrimination via product design, not illegal price-setting
  • American: $1.4B incremental revenue over 3 years (~$500M/yr). Hotels $300M+, National Car Rental saved from liquidation, broadcast networks $1B+

The VacationAir model

  • Hub-and-spoke: Pittsburgh + Newark → Charlotte (hub) → Myrtle Beach + Orlando; 2 aircraft, 4 legs, 16 ODIFs (itinerary × Y/Q fare class)
  • Decision: booking limit per ODIF · Objective: max revenue = SUMPRODUCT(fares, limits)
  • Constraints: limit ≤ demand forecast; seats per leg ≤ capacity — a connecting passenger consumes a seat on two legs
Greedy vs LP — the punchline

Greedy (fill highest fares first): $159,325. LP optimal: $169,875 — 6.6% more revenue, a huge profit swing at near-zero airline margins. Greedy fails because cheap single-leg tickets consume less capacity than expensive connecting ones; the LP rations the cheapest Q fares on full legs to protect high-yield connections.

When does RM apply? (all four must hold)

  1. High fixed costs — cost sunk regardless of occupancy
  2. Fixed, perishable capacity — empty seat worth $0 after departure
  3. Capacity can't expand in the short run
  4. Heterogeneous willingness to pay across segments

Applies to: airlines, hotels, car rentals, broadcast, sports, fine dining, fashion. Zara: fixed seasonal production run, no replenishment — RM pricing added 3–4% revenue.

Implementation & extensions

  • Rolling re-optimization: solve 6 months out → monthly → weekly → daily → every few days in the final week (cancellations & no-shows)
  • Overbooking: 10–15% no-show rate; model no-shows as a random variable, balance denied-boarding cost vs empty seats; approximation = capacity × (1 + no-show rate). United 2017 forced-removal incident (−$100M+ stock in days) → modern fix: auction voluntary rebooking at the gate, raising the offer until someone accepts
  • Assumptions to challenge: demand known exactly · prices fixed · everyone shows up · no cancellations/misconnects
Integer & Binary Programming
🔢
IP / Binary
Integer Programming — Yes/No Decisions
▾

LPs allow fractional answers; many real decisions are whole-number or yes/no. Binary (0/1) variables encode them — and simple linear constraints on binaries encode surprisingly rich logic.

Capital budgeting (7 projects, $15K budget)

  • Projects indivisible — fund fully or not at all; xi ∈ {0,1}; some ROIs exceed 200–300%, budget is binding
  • Objective = SUMPRODUCT(NPV, x) — linear ✓
  • The IF-statement version is nonlinear (piecewise) — GRG reports a wrong answer as "optimal." Use Simplex LP + bin constraints
  • Tolerance trap: Solver's default Integer Optimality is 1% — it stops within 1% of optimum. Set to 0% for the true optimum (slower); real-time systems deliberately use looser tolerance

Logical constraints with binaries (memorize)

Business ruleConstraint
If 4 then 5x₄ − x₅ ≤ 0 (i.e., x₅ ≥ x₄)
Both or neitherx₄ = x₅
Exactly one of 1,2,3x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = 1
At most 3 of 7x₁ + … + x₇ ≤ 3
At least one of 4,5x₄ + x₅ ≥ 1
Synergy bonus if 1 & 3add binary S with S ≤ x₁, S ≤ x₃; +bonus·S in objective

Ride-hailing matching (Didi, ~2016)

  • 4 users × 6 cars = 24 binary xij; each user exactly 1 car (Σ = 1), each car ≤ 1 user (Σ ≤ 1); some pairs excluded
  • Objective choice is a business decision: min total wait vs max matches — growth-stage Didi chose max matches for market share, weighted by driver acceptance likelihood
  • City scale: ~1,000 requests × 10,000 drivers = 10 million binary variables, re-solved every 2 seconds
Why IP is fundamentally harder than LP

LP feasible regions are convex — any point between two feasible points is feasible. Integer constraints destroy convexity. Rounding is fine for big numbers (16.2 → 16) but catastrophic for binaries (0.5 → 0 or 1 are completely different decisions). That's why IPs can take hours where LPs take seconds.

Decisions Under Uncertainty — Bridge to Day 5
📦
Uncertainty
Newsvendor Setup & Demand Uncertainty
▾

New product: price $100, cost $75, salvage $25; ML forecast = 200 units. How many to order before seeing demand?

Error analysis on 500 historical items

  • Actual vs forecast scatter: error grows with forecast magnitude → use the ratio (actual/forecast), not absolute error
  • Ratios span ≈ 0.5–1.5 with a ≈ flat histogram → Uniform is the right uncertainty model → demand ~ Uniform[100, 300]

Immediate implications

  • Never order above 300 (guaranteed leftover on every extra unit) or below 100 (guaranteed lost sales)
  • Intuition says slightly below 200 — correct, because overage cost ($50) > underage cost ($25)

Two questions before deciding: (1) the risk profile of a fixed decision under uncertainty, (2) which order quantity optimizes the chosen metric. Scenario analysis (100/200/300) is a start, but probabilities matter — tomorrow: Monte Carlo with 10,000+ scenarios, plus simulating the bond portfolio's default tail the LP ignored.

Flaw of Averages

E[f(X)] ≠ f(E[X]). Plugging average demand (200) into the profit model says $5,000; true expected profit at Q=200 ≈ $3,116. Averages lie when payoffs are asymmetric.

Morning — Binary Variables in the Wild
🚗
Assignment Models
Matching, Infeasibility & Fairness
▾

The car/rider matching problem: a binary variable per rider–car pair, each rider matched exactly once (row sums = 1), each car used at most once (column sums ≤ 1), minimize total waiting time.

Modeling infeasible pairs

Add an allowed-pair indicator table (1 = allowed, 0 = not) and constrain decision ≤ indicator — a 0 in the table forces the match variable to 0. Same eligibility trick as the school-bus no-bid cells.

Validation: efficiency vs fairness

Minimizing total wait can leave one rider waiting far longer than everyone else — efficient system-wide, unfair individually. Fix: compute each rider's wait and add a max-wait constraint. In class this cut the longest wait without worsening the total.

What validation can surface (Uber discussion)

  • Rider fares are fixed once requested → platform revenue may not depend on the assignment at all
  • But driver income does — minimum-daily-income guarantees can justify steering a high-fare ride to the driver furthest below the floor
  • Same structure everywhere: school buses, eHarmony-style matching, staffing experts to teams — pairwise binaries + score per pair + caps per side

GreenField Extension
Cardinality & Minimum-Threshold Constraints
▾

New requirements on the bond portfolio: limit how many bonds, and if a bond is in, hold a meaningful position. Counting positives with COUNTIF is nonlinear — binaries keep it linear.

Business ruleLinear form (N = binary "included")
If weight > 0, bond is "in"; if out, weight = 0I ≤ N (per bond)
If in, invest at least 15%I ≥ 0.15 · N
At most K bondsSUM(N) ≤ K
Class result

With the 15% minimum (and per-bond 20% cap), the optimum jumped from the six-bond 5.395% portfolio to five bonds at exactly 20% each → 5.56% — Bank, Software, Auto Mfr, Construction, Luxury. The whiteboard logic: "if I > 0 then N = 1; if N = 0 then I = 0".

In-Class Case
TikTalk 2.0 Feature Selection
▾

Pick from 12 candidate features under dev (≤28 wks), design (≤15 wks), and cloud (≤$80k/mo) capacity, plus business rules — one binary per feature.

The constraint catalog

  • Dependency: Live Shopping / Ad Targeting require Next-Gen Rec → x ≤ x_rec
  • Conflict: Stories vs Long-Form, Duets+ vs Collab Rooms → x₁ + x₂ ≤ 1
  • Required category: at least one safety feature → x₃ + x₁₁ ≥ 1
  • Conditional cap (the star): 3 experimental features normally, 5 if the Experiment Platform ships → Σ experimental ≤ 3 + 2·x₁₂. A decision variable on the RHS makes the limit conditional while staying linear.

Multiple objectives → weights

Adding acquisition + engagement + revenue treats all goals equally. Class weights: Acq 40% / Eng 40% / Rev 20% — a startup would tilt to acquisition, a public company to revenue. Sensitivity-test the weights.

Hard vs soft constraints

Hard: capacities, budget, safety, dependencies, conflicts — must hold. Soft: the risk threshold — the case gives no number, so solve with hard constraints first, look at the resulting risk score, then discuss whether to tighten. Constraints can be negotiable business positions, not laws of physics.

Nonlinear
Local vs Global Optima — and Convexity
▾
  • GRG Nonlinear climbs from wherever it starts → different starting points can land on different local optima (polynomial demo). Solver saying "optimal" may mean locally optimal.
  • Prefer linear reformulation when possible — most IF-driven "nonlinearity" converts to binaries.
  • Convex exception: minimize a convex function (or maximize a concave one) and local = global — GRG is then trustworthy. This is exactly why the Markowitz variance minimization below is safe.
Midday — Mean-Variance Portfolio
📈
Markowitz
Mean-Variance Model (Robo-Advisor Engine)
▾

Robo-advisors asking "horizon + risk tolerance" are running Markowitz mean-variance underneath: weights as decisions, expected return as reward, variance as risk.

The class build (150 months, funds AG / FC / TV)

  • Means 10.07% / 6.01% / 6.50%; stdevs 30.37% / 19.78% / 9.01%; covariance matrix via COVARIANCE.P — all pairwise covariances negative → great diversification
  • Portfolio return =SUMPRODUCT(μ, w); variance =MMULT(MMULT(w,Σ),TRANSPOSE(w)); stdev =SQRT(var)
  • Solve: min stdev, s.t. return ≥ 8%, weights sum to 1, w ≥ 0 — GRG (convex min → global)
  • Start 30/30/40: return 7.42% (infeasible), sd 9.47% → optimum AG 44.2 / FC 16.0 / TV 39.8: return exactly 8%, sd 12.90%. Buying the extra return costs volatility; the negative correlations contain the damage.

Four ways to pose the same trade-off

  1. Max return s.t. risk ≤ cap
  2. Min stdev s.t. return ≥ target (class version)
  3. Max return − λ·risk
  4. Max Sharpe ratio = (return − r_f) / stdev

A low-return asset can still earn its place if it moves differently from the rest — covariance is the point, not standalone performance.

Newsvendor Enumeration — the Profit Identity
▾
The whiteboard identity (memorize — post-module bait)

Profit = Revenue − Ordering cost + Salvage value
Revenue = Price × Units sold · Units sold = MIN(Demand, Q)
Ordering cost = cost/unit × Q · Salvage = salvage/unit × Unsold · Unsold = Q − Units sold

Price $100, cost $75, salvage $25, Demand ~ Uniform[100, 300]. Enumerate all 201 demand scenarios for a fixed Q → full profit distribution, no randomness needed.

QAvg profitWorst caseP(loss)
200 (= forecast)$3,115.67−$2,50016.9%
166 (optimal)$3,325.00−$8005.5%
150 (zero-risk)$3,274.25$00%

Why 166: critical ratio Cu/(Cu+Co) = 25/75 = 1/3 → order at the 33rd percentile of demand (166⅔). Ordering the mean overshoots because an unsold unit costs $50 while a missed sale costs $25. Higher Q raises the best case and the downside.

🎲
Method
From Enumeration to Monte Carlo
▾

Enumeration works for one discrete uncertain input. With continuous distributions or several uncertain variables, the scenario space explodes — so sample instead of enumerate.

  1. Define uncertain inputs → 2. choose distributions → 3. generate random samples → 4. compute the output per sample → 5. analyze the output distribution

Crystal Ball workflow (same as Day 4)

Define Assumption (e.g. =CB.Uniform(100,300)) → Define Forecast (profit) → Run Preferences 10,000 trials → Run → read histogram, statistics, percentiles, certainty. Reset before re-running.

AI-assisted modeling note

A clear prompt (business logic + inputs + distributions + #samples + desired outputs) got an AI to build the same sim — but it defaulted to a continuous uniform where CB used discrete. Small numeric differences, same insight. Lesson: specify distributions explicitly; validate what the tool assumed.

Two Sims
Retirement: Decumulation & Accumulation
▾

Decumulation (drawdown)

$2M at retirement, S&P mean 8.61% (sd ~19%), 20 equal withdrawals → deterministic PMT says $196K/yr lands exactly at $0 in year 20. With random returns, sequence-of-returns volatility creates a serious chance of running out years early. The sd being twice the mean is the tell.

Accumulation (Build Your Own Model — slide 61)

  • $0 start at 30, $16,845 at the start of each year, return ~ Normal(6%, 2%) — a fresh draw per year, retire at 65
  • Row logic: End = (Begin + Contribution) × (1 + r); forecast = ending balance at 64
  • Deterministic at flat 6%: $1.99M — just under the $2M goal. Simulated: P(≥ $2M) ≈ 45%; 90% of outcomes in ~$1.73M–$2.27M
Pitfall

One return cell referenced 35 times = "every year earns the same random return" — wrong model, inflated spread. Each year gets its own CB.Normal.

Risk pooling (PDF 6 callback)

Pool many independent risks and individual randomness cancels: casinos, insurers, loan books. One gambler is a coin flip; a million plays is a business. Sum sd grows as σ√n while the mean grows as nμ — relative risk shrinks.

Capstone Example
Credit Risk Simulation & Value-at-Risk
▾

200 loans (~$528M exposure), rating-based default probabilities, recovery ~ Triangular(0%, 25%, 90%).

The wrong model vs the right one

Wrong: loss = exposure × default probability → every loan "loses" a little every trial; no tail. Right: simulate the event — CB.YesNo(PD) × exposure × (1 − recovery) — no default means zero loss; a default means a big one.

  • Portfolio loss = SUM of 200 loan losses (forecast cell)
  • Distribution: heavily right-skewed; ~9% of trials lose nothing; mean ≈ $4.4M
  • 95% VaR ≈ $11.1M (95th percentile), 99% VaR ≈ $14.3M

VaR phrasing (exam-grade): "We are α% certain the portfolio will not lose more than $V over horizon N." It emerged from Bernoulli × Triangular products — no normal assumption could price that tail.
